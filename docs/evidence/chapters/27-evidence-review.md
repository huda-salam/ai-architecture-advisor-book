## Important evidence boundaries

### 1. Calibration is not the same as discrimination

A model can rank risk effectively while its numerical probabilities are poorly calibrated. The chapter therefore does not use AUC or another discrimination metric as evidence that a displayed percentage is trustworthy.

### 2. “68%” is an example, not empirical evidence

The 68% figure is a deliberately illustrative AI-IDSS example. It is **not** a claim about any real portfolio company and is not presented as a validated prediction.

### 3. 60% threshold is an example policy

The 60% escalation threshold is an illustrative policy assumption. It is not a recommended universal threshold and should not be interpreted as an industry standard.

### 4. LLM confidence claim is deliberately limited

The chapter does not claim that LLMs can never be calibrated. The defensible claim is narrower: a confidence-like number generated by an LLM must not be represented as a calibrated event probability unless the specific system has been evaluated for that purpose.

### 5. Human decision boundary is context-specific

The chapter recommends explicit human authority for consequential investment decisions because the system is an AI investment decision-support system. This is an architecture recommendation, not a universal proposition that humans must always approve every AI output.

### 6. NIST version awareness

NIST states that AI RMF 1.0 is being revised. References should therefore identify the version used and should not imply that AI RMF 1.0 is immutable or the final state of the framework.

## Adversarial review questions

- Could the displayed percentage actually be a heuristic score?
- Could future information leak into historical training features?
- Could calibration deteriorate after deployment?
- Could a threshold be selected to generate a desired alert volume rather than from decision consequences?
- Could the LLM explanation be mistaken for primary evidence?
- Could an alert be generated outside the model's validated population?
- Could missing or stale data produce a confident-looking result?
- Can the organization reconstruct why a particular alert was generated?
- Is the recommendation being confused with the prediction?
- Does the architecture have an explicit insufficient-evidence state?

## Falsifiability

The chapter's main recommendations should change if evidence demonstrates that:

1. a specific LLM-based probability mechanism is reliably calibrated on a representative investment-risk population;
2. a simpler rule-based system produces equal or superior decision utility with better auditability;
3. the historical data is insufficient to support probability estimation;
4. the cost structure makes a different alert threshold optimal;
5. post-deployment monitoring demonstrates stable calibration and validity under the actual operating distribution.

## Editorial conclusion

**Status: ACCEPTED FOR CURRENT REVIEW SCOPE.**

The chapter is intentionally conservative about numerical risk claims. It distinguishes prediction from recommendation, probability from score, evidence from explanation, and model output from decision authority. No universal claim is made that a particular model family, calibration method, threshold, or LLM architecture is mandatory.
